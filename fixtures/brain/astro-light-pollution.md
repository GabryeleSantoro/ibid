# Light Pollution and Dark Skies

## The Bortle scale

The Bortle scale rates how dark a sky is, from class 1, a pristine site where the Milky Way casts shadows, to class 9, an inner-city sky where only the Moon, the planets and a handful of bright stars show through the glow. Most suburbs sit at class 5 or 6. Each step down the scale reveals more: at class 4 the Milky Way is obvious overhead, at class 3 the zodiacal light appears after dusk.

## Finding a dark-sky site

Driving even an hour away from town can drop the sky by two or three Bortle classes. Light-pollution maps show the brightness of the sky at night across the country. Official dark-sky sites and parks have lighting rules and often car parks set aside for observers. Look for a spot with a low southern horizon, away from roads where headlights sweep across the field.

## Keep your night vision

Your eyes take about twenty minutes to adapt fully to the dark, and one glance at a white phone screen undoes it. Use red torches: red light barely affects the dark-adapted eye, so you can read charts and change eyepieces without losing faint detail. Turn phone screens to a red night mode.
